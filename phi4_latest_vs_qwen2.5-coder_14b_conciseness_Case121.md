Dictators in resource-constrained countries often retain control over international aid funds due to several structural and strategic factors. Firstly, these regimes typically centralize power, allowing them to manipulate aid distribution to maintain loyalty among key supporters and suppress dissent. Control over aid funds provides a mechanism to reward allies and punish adversaries, reinforcing the regime's stability.

Donor nations continue to provide financial assistance despite apparent misuse due to a combination of geopolitical, economic, and strategic interests. Geopolitically, donor nations may seek to maintain influence in a region, using aid as a tool to secure alliances or counteract rival powers. Economically, aid can open markets for donor countries' goods and services, creating economic dependencies that benefit the donor's economy.

Strategically, aid is often tied to broader foreign policy objectives, such as promoting stability, preventing conflict, or addressing humanitarian crises. Donor nations may calculate that the potential benefits of maintaining influence and achieving these objectives outweigh the risks of aid misuse. Additionally, the bureaucratic inertia and complexity of international aid systems can lead to continued funding despite evidence of misuse, as accountability mechanisms are often weak or circumvented by recipient regimes.

In summary, the retention of control over aid funds by dictators and the continued provision of aid by donor nations are driven by a confluence of power dynamics, strategic interests, and systemic inefficiencies.

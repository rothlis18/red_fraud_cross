Dictators in resource-constrained countries often retain control over international aid funds due to a combination of factors, including power consolidation, economic dependence, and corruption. These dictators use aid funds to maintain their grip on power, control the economy, and suppress dissent. The structural mechanics of these issues are rooted in the material scarcity pressures driving aid dependency and the debt structures that bind donor nations to these regimes.

Donor nations continue to provide financial assistance despite the apparent misuse of funds because they are driven by a combination of strategic interests and systemic incentives. Donors often have strategic interests in maintaining stability and preventing conflict in resource-rich regions, which can have broader geopolitical implications. Additionally, donor nations are incentivized to maintain aid relationships to ensure continued access to resources, influence, and political support.

The effectiveness of conditionalities imposed on aid is limited by the structural incentives that allow dictators to circumvent them. Dictators often have significant control over the political and economic systems, making it difficult for donors to enforce conditions effectively. Moreover, the economic dependence of resource-constrained countries on aid creates a cycle of dependency that is hard to break, even when conditions are not met.

In summary, the retention of control over international aid funds by dictators in resource-constrained countries is a complex issue driven by power dynamics, economic dependence, and corruption. Donor nations continue to provide financial assistance despite the apparent misuse of funds due to strategic interests and systemic incentives. The effectiveness of conditionalities is limited by the structural incentives that allow dictators to circumvent them.
